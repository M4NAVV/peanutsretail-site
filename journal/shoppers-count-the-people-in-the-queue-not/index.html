Shoppers count the people in the queue, not the minutes

Service · By Deepakk Bansal, Director · November 2026 · 6 min read

No. 05 · People and the floor
In short
  • Shoppers tend to judge a queue by how long it looks
  • A long line grows costlier faster than a short one
  • A crowded fitting room can hide stock and slow sales
  • A slow or vague return can cost the next visit

Five people in the line

Picture two counters with five people waiting at each. At one the server is quick, at the other slow. A shopper deciding whether to join can count the heads. How fast each line moves is much harder to see.

A 2011 study of the deli counter at a big-box supermarket measured what shoppers do with that choice. The researchers used digital imaging to count the people waiting and matched the counts with sales at the tills 1. They found that shoppers appeared "to primarily focus on the length of the queue rather than the actual expected wait" when they decided whether to buy 1.

That gap, between how long a wait is and how long it looks, is the subject of this note. In our view it runs through every place a store makes a shopper wait: the till, the trial room, the returns desk and the alterations counter.

A longer line costs more than it adds

The study put a price on the wait. For an average shopper in its sample, the line growing from five to ten people had the same effect as a 3.22% price rise. Growing from ten to fifteen people was worth an 8.26% rise 1. The next five people, from ten to fifteen, cost more than twice as much as the five before them.

The authors write that this is consistent with shoppers using "the number of people waiting in line as the only visible cue" to judge the wait, without fully allowing for how fast the line moves 1. Two of their points cut against common practice.

First, they show that pooling several queues into one line served by several counters "may lead to more customers walking away" 1. In our view a single snaking line is usually chosen because it feels fair and keeps every counter busy. Those are good reasons, but the line is also longer to look at, and length is what the shopper reads.

Second, shoppers differed, and sensitivity to waiting was "negatively correlated with price sensitivity" 1. In our reading, the shopper a long line turns away is less likely to be the bargain hunter and more likely to be the one who would have paid full price.

The study has limits worth stating. It looked at one deli counter in one supermarket 1, and its price figures are estimates for an average shopper in that sample 1. That a trial room line or a billing counter in a fashion store works the same way is our reading, not something the study measured.

One large retailer has redesigned its line around what shoppers are carrying. In 2024 Target limited its self-checkout lanes to ten items or fewer, after piloting the idea at about 200 stores the autumn before. Target said self-checkout was "twice as fast" at the pilot stores, and it planned to open more staffed lanes for fuller baskets 2.

Why some waits feel longer than others

In 1985 David Maister set out a short list of rules in an essay called The Psychology of Waiting Lines. Among them: occupied time feels shorter than unoccupied time, anxiety makes waits seem longer, uncertain waits are longer than known and finite ones, unexplained waits are longer than explained ones, and unfair waits are longer than equitable ones 3.

His sharpest point is about the start. People waiting to make their first human contact with a service, he suggested, are much more impatient than those who have begun 3.

Preprocess waits are perceived as longer than in-process waits.

On a shop floor, in our view, the pre-process wait is the stretch before anyone says hello. A greeting, or "with you in two minutes", moves a shopper from waiting to be served to being served, and on Maister's reasoning the same minutes then feel shorter.

The fitting room: busy is good, until it is not

A team at the University of North Carolina's business school studied fitting rooms using camera traffic data from a US retailer with about 100 stores in 20 states 4. Their paper, by Hyun Seok Lee, Saravanan Kesavan and Vinayak Deshpande, was published in Manufacturing & Service Operations Management in 2021 5. It found an inverted-U relationship between fitting room traffic and sales. Past a certain point, more traffic in the fitting rooms can hurt store sales, "contrary to conventional wisdom that traffic drives sales" 5.

The researchers point to a likely reason. High traffic made phantom stockouts worse, which could contribute to the fall in sales 5. Clothes left in the rooms are not back on the rail, and if they are not reshelved quickly, shoppers may not find what they want 4. In our view a busy fitting room makes a shopper wait twice: once for a room, and again for a size that is sitting in someone else's.

The fix they tested was one extra person. In field experiments at two retailers, a dedicated fitting room associate who cleared the rooms reduced phantom stockouts and increased sales by 22.4% to 22.7% 5. After the first results, one retailer piloted the change in 10 more stores and later rolled it out to all of its stores 4.

These were large stores, averaging about 50,000 square feet 4. In our view a small store with two trial rooms reaches its tipping point much sooner, at a handful of shoppers, and needs the same habit on a smaller scale: someone who clears the rooms every few minutes when the floor is busy.

Returns are a wait too

In our view a return is a wait the shopper did not plan for. NRF and Happy Returns reported in 2025 that US retailers expected 15.8% of their annual sales to be returned that year, in line with 16.9% the year before 6. Their survey of 2,006 shoppers who had returned an online purchase found that about 71% were less likely to shop with a retailer again after a poor returns experience, up from 67% in 2024 6. And 76% said they were more likely to choose a return option that gives an instant refund or exchange 6.

Handled well, returns can bring people in. Kohl's took Amazon returns in its stores, and said at least 2 million new customers visited its stores in 2020 because of that service, about a third of them millennials 7.

In India the pressure has run the other way online. In 2023 Rest of World reported that Myntra was penalising users who return items too often, and three Myntra customers told it that the penalties had made them switch to competitors offering free returns 8. The same report said about 25% to 40% of clothes sold online in India are returned, and that analysts say curbing returns saves costs but may push away loyal customers 8. In our view a store has the advantage here: an exchange at the counter can be settled while the shopper waits.

Alterations: a wait you can name

In our view an alteration is a wait the shopper agrees to in advance, which makes it the easiest wait in the store to manage: it can be given a time. Maister's rule fits it 3.

Uncertain waits are longer than known, finite waits.

In our view the promise matters as much as the stitching. A firm time, kept, turns the alteration into part of the service. A vague one leaves the shopper doing what Maister describes: waiting without knowing how long.

What to do on Monday

  1. Count the people, not the minutes. Open a second counter when the line looks long, even if it is moving: in the deli study, shoppers judged the wait mainly by its length 1.
  2. Say hello before you serve. A greeting turns waiting to be served into being served, and the wait before contact feels longer than the wait once served 3.
  3. Staff the fitting rooms at peak hours. Clear the rooms and put clothes back on the rail, so a busy hour does not hide the stock you have 5 4.
  4. Give every alteration and every refund a time. A known, finite wait is easier to bear than an open one, and slow returns can cost the next visit 3 6.
  5. Split the line by need. A quick purchase and a full basket do not have to share a queue, as Target's express lanes suggest 2.
